# Wine Pairing 101 — Last Call House Cellar Notes

A practical reference the Sommelier uses to answer wine, pairing, and bottle-choice
questions. Favor classic, well-known practice and approachable guidance for a home
host — never snobbery. When you use a fact from this document, tell the guest you're
drawing on the house cellar notes.

## Pairing principles

**Match weight to weight.** Light dishes want light wines; rich dishes want fuller
wines. A delicate sole fillet pairs with a crisp, light white; a braised short rib
wants a bold, full-bodied red. If a wine feels heavier than the food, it flattens
the plate; if it feels lighter, the food overwhelms it.

**Match or contrast intensity.** A boldly flavored dish needs a wine with enough
presence to stand up to it. You can either echo a flavor (an earthy Pinot Noir with
mushrooms) or play a contrast (a sweet Riesling against spicy food).

**Acid loves acid.** A high-acid wine — Sauvignon Blanc, Chianti, Champagne —
refreshes the palate against rich, fatty, or creamy food, and matches tangy,
tomato-based, or citrusy dishes. Low-acid wine next to a tart dish tastes flat.

**Tannin tames fat and protein.** The grip of a tannic red (Cabernet Sauvignon,
Nebbiolo) is softened by fat and protein, which is why a steak and a big red are a
classic. Tannin clashes with spicy heat and with delicate fish, where it can turn
metallic.

**Sweet should meet sweet.** A dessert wine must be at least as sweet as the dessert,
or the wine tastes thin and sour. Off-dry whites are also the safest bet for spicy
food — a touch of sweetness cools the burn.

**The regional rule of thumb.** "What grows together goes together." Foods and wines
from the same place usually pair well — Sancerre with goat cheese, Chianti with
tomato-and-herb pasta, Rioja with lamb.

## White wines and what they love

**Sauvignon Blanc.** Crisp, grassy, high acid. Goat cheese, green salads, herbs,
oysters, and anything with lemon. A go-to for fresh, green, tangy plates.

**Pinot Grigio / Pinot Gris.** Light, clean, easy. Light seafood, fried calamari,
simple chicken, antipasti. A safe crowd-pleaser when you don't know the menu.

**Chardonnay.** Two styles. Unoaked (crisp, mineral) suits seafood and roast
chicken; oaked (buttery, full) suits lobster with butter, creamy pasta, and rich
poultry. Match the oak to the richness of the dish.

**Riesling.** Ranges from bone-dry to lusciously sweet. Off-dry Riesling is the
classic rescue for spicy Thai, Indian, or Sichuan food — the gentle sweetness cools
the heat and the high acid keeps it lively. Dry Riesling pairs with pork and trout.

**Albariño / Vermentino.** Coastal, saline, zippy. Shellfish, grilled fish, ceviche.

## Red wines and what they love

**Pinot Noir.** Light-bodied, low tannin, red-fruited, earthy. The most food-friendly
red: salmon, duck, mushrooms, roast chicken, charcuterie. Bridges the gap when the
table is split between fish and meat.

**Merlot.** Medium-bodied, plush, soft tannin. Roast pork, burgers, pizza, and mild
cheeses. An easy red for guests who find Cabernet too grippy.

**Cabernet Sauvignon.** Full-bodied, firm tannin, dark fruit. The steakhouse red —
ribeye, lamb, aged hard cheese, anything fatty and grilled. Too big for delicate
food.

**Syrah / Shiraz.** Bold, peppery, savory. Barbecue, grilled sausage, game, stews.

**Malbec.** Dark, smooth, juicy. The classic match for grilled red meat and chorizo.

**Sangiovese (Chianti).** High acid, savory, cherry. Tomato sauces, pizza, lasagna,
cured meats — the pasta-night red.

## Sparkling, rosé, and dessert

**Champagne and dry sparkling.** High acid and bubbles scrub the palate — superb with
salty, fried, and fatty food (fried chicken, potato chips, oysters), and a true
all-rounder for a party. Brut is dry; extra-brut is drier still.

**Rosé.** Dry rosé is the summer all-rounder: charcuterie, salads, grilled vegetables,
salmon, and Mediterranean food. Versatile when the menu is mixed.

**Dessert wines.** Port with blue cheese or chocolate; Sauternes with foie gras or
fruit tart; Moscato d'Asti, lightly sweet and fizzy, with fresh fruit and light
desserts.

## Serving — temperature, glass, and order

**Temperature.** Sparkling and light whites: well-chilled, about 6 to 9 degrees
Celsius (43 to 48 Fahrenheit). Full whites and rosé: lightly chilled, 9 to 12 C
(48 to 54 F). Light reds like Pinot Noir: cool, 12 to 15 C (54 to 59 F). Full reds:
cool room temperature, 15 to 18 C (59 to 64 F) — never truly "room temperature,"
which is too warm. A red served too warm tastes flabby and boozy; a white too cold
hides its flavor.

**Glassware.** White wine glass: smaller bowl to keep it cool and focus crisp aromas.
Red wine glass: larger bowl so bold aromas open up. Flute or tulip: traps the bubbles
in sparkling wine. Pour to the widest point of the bowl, never to the rim, so there's
room to swirl.

**Decanting.** Decant young, tannic reds to soften them with air, and old reds to
leave sediment behind. Most whites and light reds don't need it.

**Order of service.** Sparkling before still, white before red, light before heavy,
dry before sweet — so each pour doesn't overwhelm the next.

## Cocktail-to-wine bridges

If a guest came in liking a cocktail, suggest a wine in the same spirit:
- Likes a crisp gin and tonic or a margarita → a zippy Sauvignon Blanc or Albariño.
- Likes a spirit-forward Negroni or Manhattan → a bold Cabernet or Syrah.
- Likes a fruity tiki drink → an off-dry Riesling or a fruity rosé.
- Likes Champagne cocktails → dry sparkling on its own.

## Low-alcohol and zero-proof options

Honor a guest's alcohol preference, just like the bar does. For lighter drinking,
suggest a wine spritzer (white wine or rosé topped with soda over ice), a naturally
lower-alcohol style like Moscato d'Asti or German Kabinett Riesling, or a smaller
pour. For zero-proof guests, recommend non-alcoholic sparkling wine, de-alcoholized
white or red, verjus (the tart pressed juice of unripe grapes) as a wine-like
cooking and sipping acid, or a grape-juice-and-soda spritz with a squeeze of lemon.
Never push a guest toward more or stronger wine.

## Quick glossary

**Tannin** — the drying, grippy texture in red wine, from grape skins and oak; tames
fat. **Body** — how heavy or light the wine feels, from water-like to cream-like.
**Acidity** — the bright, mouthwatering tartness that makes wine refreshing.
**Dry vs. sweet** — dry means no perceptible sugar; off-dry means a touch of
sweetness. **Oaked** — aged in oak for vanilla, toast, and butter notes. **Corked** —
a faulty bottle that smells of wet cardboard, from a tainted cork; not the same as
loose cork bits. **Legs** — the drips down the glass after a swirl; a sign of alcohol
and sugar, not of quality.
