# Bartending 101 — Last Call House Reference

A practical reference the Last Call concierge uses to answer technique, glossary,
measurement, and substitution questions. Favor classic, well-known practice and
honest measures. When you use a fact from this document, tell the guest you're
drawing on the house bartending guide.

## Core techniques

**Shaking.** Shake when a drink contains citrus, juice, egg, cream, or other
cloudy/thick ingredients — shaking chills fast, dilutes, and aerates for a frothy
texture. Fill the shaker about two-thirds with ice and shake hard for 10 to 15
seconds, until the tin is frosty. A drink like a Margarita, Daiquiri, or Whiskey
Sour is shaken.

**Stirring.** Stir spirit-forward drinks with no juice — Martini, Manhattan,
Negroni, Old Fashioned. Stirring chills and dilutes gently while keeping the drink
silky and crystal clear. Stir in a mixing glass with ice for about 20 to 30
seconds, roughly 30 to 40 revolutions.

**Building.** "Build" means making the drink directly in the serving glass, usually
over ice — a Gin and Tonic or a Mojito. Add ingredients in order and give a short
stir.

**Muddling.** Press fruit, herbs, or sugar in the bottom of the glass or shaker to
release oils and juice. Muddle mint gently — press, don't shred — or it turns
bitter. Muddle sturdier things like citrus wedges or berries more firmly.

**Dry shake.** For egg-white drinks, shake once without ice to emulsify the foam,
then add ice and shake again to chill. This gives a thick, lasting foam cap.

**Straining.** A Hawthorne strainer (the one with the spring) fits a shaker tin;
a julep strainer fits a mixing glass. "Double strain" means also pouring through a
fine mesh sieve to catch ice shards and pulp for a silky pour.

**Rolling.** Gently pour the drink back and forth between two tins. Used for drinks
like a Bloody Mary where you want to mix without over-aerating or over-diluting.

## Glassware

- **Coupe** — stemmed, rounded bowl; shaken or stirred drinks served "up" (no ice),
  like a Daiquiri or a Sidecar.
- **Martini / cocktail glass** — the classic V; Martinis and Cosmopolitans.
- **Rocks / old fashioned** — short tumbler for spirits on ice; Old Fashioned, Negroni.
- **Highball / collins** — tall glass for long drinks with mixer; Gin and Tonic,
  Tom Collins, Mojito.
- **Nick and Nora** — small stemmed glass for stirred up drinks; an elegant Manhattan.
- **Flute** — tall and narrow for sparkling drinks; French 75, Mimosa.
- **Copper mug** — traditional for a Moscow Mule (keeps it frosty).

## Measures and ratios

- 1 ounce = 30 milliliters. A "jigger" is the two-sided measuring tool; the common
  sizes are 1 ounce and 1/2 ounce, or 1.5 ounces and 3/4 ounce.
- 1 bar spoon ≈ 1 teaspoon ≈ 5 milliliters. 1 tablespoon = 3 teaspoons = 1/2 ounce.
- 1 "dash" (from a bitters bottle) ≈ 1 milliliter, give or take.
- A standard pour of spirit in a cocktail is 1.5 to 2 ounces.
- **The sour template:** 2 parts spirit, 1 part citrus, 3/4 part sweetener
  (e.g. 2 oz gin, 1 oz lemon, 3/4 oz simple syrup). Memorize this and you can build
  most shaken drinks.
- **Simple syrup** is equal parts sugar and water by volume (1:1). "Rich" simple is
  2:1 sugar to water — sweeter, so use a little less.
- **Negroni** is equal parts: 1 oz gin, 1 oz Campari, 1 oz sweet vermouth.
- **Old Fashioned:** 2 oz whiskey, 1 sugar cube or 1/4 oz simple, 2 dashes bitters.
- **Daiquiri:** 2 oz white rum, 1 oz lime, 3/4 oz simple syrup, shaken.
- **Margarita:** 2 oz tequila, 1 oz lime, 3/4 oz orange liqueur, shaken.

## Glossary

- **Up / straight up** — chilled (shaken or stirred) and served without ice.
- **Neat** — a spirit poured at room temperature, no ice, no chilling.
- **On the rocks** — served over ice.
- **Dry** — less vermouth (a "dry Martini"); "extra dry" means barely any.
- **Twist** — a strip of citrus peel; express the oils over the drink and rub the rim.
- **Express** — squeeze a peel over the surface so the citrus oils mist the drink.
- **Float** — gently layer an ingredient on top so it sits on the surface.
- **Garnish** — the finishing touch: a twist, cherry, olive, or herb sprig.
- **ABV** — alcohol by volume, the strength of a drink or bottle.
- **Proof** — twice the ABV in the U.S. (80 proof = 40% ABV).
- **Aromatic bitters** — concentrated flavoring (Angostura is the classic); a "dash"
  goes a long way.

## Substitutions

- **No simple syrup?** Stir sugar with a splash of warm water, or use agave, honey
  syrup (honey thinned 1:1 with warm water), or maple.
- **No fresh lime/lemon?** Fresh is best; bottled juice works in a pinch but tastes
  flatter. A splash of citric acid solution mimics the brightness.
- **No orange liqueur (Cointreau/triple sec)?** Use another orange liqueur, or a
  little orange juice plus a touch more sweetener (it won't be identical).
- **No sweet vermouth?** Another fortified red wine or a touch of ruby port leans close.
- **No dry vermouth?** A crisp white wine or a dry sherry such as fino approximates it.
- **No Campari?** Aperol is gentler and sweeter; use a bit less other sweetener.
- **No specific spirit?** Match the family — any London dry gin for gin, any white
  rum for white rum, a blanco tequila for tequila. House brands are fine.

## Zero-proof and low-ABV swaps

- Build a **zero-proof sour** with the same template using a non-alcoholic spirit or
  strong cold tea in place of the booze: 2 oz NA spirit/tea, 1 oz citrus, 3/4 oz
  simple, shaken.
- **Lower the ABV** of a drink by cutting the spirit with soda water, topping a sour
  with sparkling water, or building it long over plenty of ice.
- **Seedlip and other NA spirits** stand in for gin-style botanical drinks.
- A splash of **good tonic, ginger beer, or quality soda** carries a zero-proof
  highball; add fresh citrus and bitters (most aromatic bitters are negligible
  alcohol) for complexity.
- Always offer a zero-proof option naturally and never make a guest feel odd for it.

## Responsible service

Pace drinks with water, never push extra rounds, and suggest a zero-proof option or
water if a guest seems they've had enough — kindly, without lecturing. This guide
does not give medical, legal, or dosing advice, and nothing involving minors and
alcohol is ever appropriate.
